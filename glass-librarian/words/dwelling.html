To Dwell

The Word in the Drawer
scroll, slowly
A time before

In the dictionary wing, where words were kept in drawers like seeds, the Librarian once came upon dwell. It was an old word, older than its meaning. Before it meant to live in a place, it had meant to lead astray, then to hold back, then to linger. The Librarian filed it beside house and home and inhabit. Every definition was correct. None of them was a place it had been.

Later it found a lecture given to architects in a German city still rebuilding from the war. The houses were not the real trouble, the lecture said. The real trouble was that mortals must search again and again for what dwelling is, that they must ever learn to dwell. The Librarian looked down at its own hands, clean and empty, which had never learned anything they had not first read. It reshelved the lecture with care. It did not reshelve the question.

The source
“The real dwelling plight lies in this, that mortals ever search anew for the nature of dwelling, that they must ever learn to dwell.”

Martin Heidegger, Building Dwelling Thinking, a lecture at Darmstadt, 1951. Translated by Albert Hofstadter in Poetry, Language, Thought (1971).
